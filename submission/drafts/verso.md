**Themes.** The book asks what a society owes a person who can offer it nothing
in return, and how that person can hold it to the promise. It opens on
Santoshi Kumari, eleven, who died in Jharkhand in 2017 after her family's
ration card was cancelled for not being linked to a biometric identity number.
Its answer is a constitution. Being a person gives standing and a floor of
nine essentials, owed by named public bodies, and work, wealth, citizenship and
good conduct are not conditions of it.

The rest of the design protects that floor: public finance without a debt
brake, property conditional on the floor and the commons, work that can be
refused without losing anything owed, records bound to their purpose,
emergencies that suspend no right, and a small core no majority may amend away.
Each chapter argues its rule against the strongest alternative, including
disenfranchisement as punishment, the debt brake and the tell-us-once state.
Each Part opens with a documented injustice. Every rule is published as
executable logic with public tests, so a reader can check the constitution's
consequences rather than trust its author.

**Markets.** The main reader is the serious non-specialist who wants to know

**Competing titles.**
- Erik Olin Wright's *Envisioning Real Utopias*, from Verso, surveys
  emancipatory alternatives. This book specifies one constitutional destination

- John Rawls's *A Theory of Justice* derives principles of justice. This book

- Elinor Ostrom's *Governing the Commons* analyses institutions empirically.
