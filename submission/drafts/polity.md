Dear George Owers,

I would like to propose *The Rights Nobody Has to Earn* for Polity. It is a
topical book with a critical edge, written for general readers, on what a
society owes the people who can give it nothing back.

**Working title.** *The Rights Nobody Has to Earn: A worked design for a
society, with its formal claims made executable*

**Synopsis.** The book opens on Santoshi Kumari, eleven, who died in Jharkhand
in 2017 after her family's ration card was cancelled for not being linked to a
biometric identity number. It asks what a society owes a person who can offer
it nothing in return, and how that person can hold it to the promise. Its
answer is a constitution. Being a person gives standing and a floor of nine
essentials, owed by named public bodies, and work, wealth, citizenship and good
conduct are not conditions of it. The rest of the design protects that floor:
- government divided by place and function;
- the vote resting on residence;
- records bound to their purpose;
- emergencies that suspend no right;
- a small core no majority may amend away.

Each chapter states its rule plainly, with cases, then argues it against the
strongest alternative: the evidence, the cost and who bears it, and what would
change the choice. Each Part opens with a documented case, among them the
Grootboom eviction, the Dutch childcare-benefits affair and *ADM Jabalpur*.
The last Part draws the choices together and ranks where the design could
fail. Every rule is also published as executable logic with public tests, so
a reader can check what the constitution entails. The book claims its rules
have the consequences it describes and argues its choices. It does not claim
that the society would work or is affordable.

**Length.** About 98,000 words, including the notes, works cited and

**Market and level.** This is a book for general readers: the serious
non-specialist interested in rights, public institutions and alternatives to
making subsistence depend on employment or approved conduct. Lawyers,
constitutional designers and policymakers are the second audience. It could
serve as supplementary reading in political theory, constitutional law and
social policy.

**Competing titles.**
- John Rawls's *A Theory of Justice* develops justice as fairness through a
  social contract. This book shares its concern with the terms of a society's
  institutions, but presents one concrete constitution through cases instead
  of deriving principles.
- Erik Olin Wright's *Envisioning Real Utopias* examines alternatives to
  capitalism. This book's object is narrower: a single constitutional
  destination, with operation and transition left to a second volume.
- Elinor Ostrom's *Governing the Commons* analyses institutions empirically.
  This book is a normative design with formal examples, and offers no
  equivalent field evidence.

What is distinctive is the combination: a sustained plain-language
constitutional argument whose every rule a reader can run and test.
