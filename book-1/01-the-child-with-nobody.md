# The Child With Nobody

The record holds one entry about Nell: Nell was born. It is silent about a
parent, family, home, household, supporter or teacher, about any case against
Nell or order placing Nell anywhere, and about whether anyone has provided
anything.

Those are absences in a record. Whether Nell has a family, where Nell lives
and what Nell feels are questions it leaves open. The question here is what
follows from the birth entry alone.

For anyone other than a public body, a recorded birth is enough for the rules
to conclude personhood. The rule asks for the birth and nothing else: a
parent, an age, a document and a match against a registry are all outside it.
Nell therefore enters the design as a person. The entries Nell lacks are
simply absent, and nothing in the rules waits for them.

## Everything owed

Every person is owed the floor: food, shelter, care, learning, bodily safety,
material security, expression, belief and company. Each duty has the same
single condition, personhood. Employment, payment, family membership and good
behaviour are outside it.

Nell is owed the whole floor. Every debt and every entitlement follows from
the birth entry. The floor is complete from the start, and a relative's
arrival or a later registration would leave it exactly as it is.

Nobody has to classify Nell as vulnerable either. The record refuses that word
outright, and an attempt to enter it is rejected. Protection follows without
it, and what is owed is the same whether a family is recorded or absent.

## No one is presumed to provide

A family entry would show nothing about whether somebody fed, housed or cared
for Nell. The rules bar any presumption that a household, relative,
association or charity supplies another person's floor. Care continuity is a
public duty, and kinship creates no personal-care debt.

Personhood also limits power. Children hold rights in their own name, and no
minimum speaking age may exclude their voice. Information and assistance must
be accessible to them; support comes before removal, and separation is a last
resort. The rules read a missing relationship as a missing entry, never as
independence or as a ground for confinement.

These limits hold for Nell, whose record is silent about a parent, household
or chosen supporter. A few rules address a particular recorded relationship,
such as a marriage or a sibling. Where that relationship is absent, the
specific rule has nothing to apply to, and the universal protections remain.

The next question is who must begin assistance without waiting for Nell to ask.

## Help that does not wait for a request

The independent rights advocate owes every person the initiation of assistance
and representation without a request. Nell is a person, so the duty follows.
Personhood is all it reads: a finding about ability, a family record, an
application or a proceeding begun by Nell would change nothing about it.

Somebody else can also raise the concern. A passerby may record that
assistance appears to be missing, and that entry gives a separate rights
office a duty to review whether it was begun. The passerby needs only to have
seen; a seat, an authorisation or a relationship to Nell is unnecessary. The
review duty is the entry's whole consequence. It records a concern, and the
rules draw from it nothing adverse about Nell, whether breach, incapacity or a
ground for punishment.

A more specific public duty needs more. In a separate care-continuity case the
record names the authorised actor and the care matter, and the duty follows
while every family role stays empty. It covers the care matter it names;
parentage and authority over the rest of the person's life remain outside it.
The chapter on roles follows those limits in detail. Nell's record lacks such
a case, so for Nell the specific duty is absent while the duty to begin
assistance stands. The requirement to begin is unconditional; a particular
exercise of care authority needs its own grounds and limits.

## What delivery needs

Nell is owed food; nothing in the record yet shows that food reached Nell. The
same distinction holds for shelter, care, material security and company.
Bodily safety is guaranteed as protection and has no delivery conclusion at
all.

For these ordinary delivery conclusions, the rules need a report of receipt
and matching independent evidence. The witness must be authorised for this
recipient and must be distinct from the provider. Nell's record contains
neither. In another case the same entries are recorded for a person outside
custody, and the delivery conclusions follow. What differs is the evidence of
receipt; the entitlement is the same.

Learning has a further route, through teaching that was heard, and Nell's
record lacks both a teacher and a hearing. Belief is protected without any
route certifying that a person believes. Custody adds duties to secure shelter
and voice; the shelter receipt and the evidence that somebody spoke remain
separate entries. Nell is outside custody and owed both protections all the
same.

## Who counts

Nell's birth is one way into the record. Personhood also follows from first
contact, from presence within the republic's jurisdiction and from being under
its effective control. Each route stands alone, and none asks for citizenship,
identity papers or a family.

The passerby's report is another way in. A report that a person appears to
have nobody acting for them is also an encounter. Whoever saw the person may
write it, and it gives standing on the same terms as first contact, with
nothing asked about the person's papers, status or history. It places nobody
in custody.

Presence reaches anyone within the jurisdiction, regardless of any permission
to be there. Effective control reaches anyone under the republic's power
wherever they are, so moving a person across a border leaves this ground of
obligation in place.

Each route leads to the same floor. Recorded for a different person each time,
every route yields the same debt of food, and the entitlements and protections
attach to the resulting personhood however it was reached. The routes differ;
the personhood they lead to is one and the same.

## A name at an encounter is a handle

An encounter needs a way to refer to the person, and a temporary name serves.
It refers and settles nothing else: civil identity, citizenship, family
relationship, liability and guilt all stay open. Assistance can begin without
a match against another record.

A claimed alias leaves identities separate. When a registry cites one alias
claim against two encounter names, neither name becomes the other, in either
direction. The claim therefore imports no conviction, sentence or history
through an assumed match. A consequential identity link stays a separate claim
that can be challenged.

The entry rules also keep people and public bodies apart. A first-contact
entry naming the Court, or a report that nobody is acting for it, leaves the
Court outside personhood, because the Court is already marked as a public body
and the entry rules exclude public bodies. Encounter entries give standing to
people only.

## What cannot take it away

A broken status record leaves a valid standing root intact. When a registry
entry about a person who entered by first contact is marked broken, the entry
stays visible as a broken entry, and personhood still follows from the first
contact. A defect in that record leaves the encounter standing.

Missing or disputed age evidence also leaves standing intact. The ballot
follows only with adulthood evidence; the floor asks only for personhood.
Missing capacity evidence and a refusal of support likewise leave personhood
where it was. These are separate questions.

Correction must keep the provenance needed for accountability and remedy.
While an identity is uncertain, each person's history stays separate and what
the unreconciled person is owed stays owed.

## Standing without a roster

Standing does not depend on a maintained roster. A recorded birth or encounter
is enough, including an encounter held under a temporary name. The floor,
process and remedy begin without waiting for identity papers.

The custody and release rules remain valid independent routes. A person whose
encounter went unrecorded keeps standing when the record begins with
confinement. Protective-order and independently witnessed physical-holding
routes provide further backstops. The prisoner chapter follows the case in
which only the conviction route is present.

Personhood can follow from a custody conclusion without proof that the
holding was entered accurately, just as it can follow from an encounter entry
without authentication of the name attached to it.

## The child the record never entered

Every way in described here needs an entry. Nell has one entry. Ori has none.

For Ori the rules conclude nothing: personhood, the floor entitlement and the
duty to begin assistance are all absent. A birth entry or an encounter entry
would open a route.

The design's alarms miss Ori as well. The markers that watch confinement have
nothing to watch. A rule flagging every recorded person without evidence of
food, tried in a separate case, reaches Nell and misses Ori, because it too
starts from a person the record can name. An entry rule, however wide, reaches
only someone who has an entry.

## What this cannot settle

An entry needs somebody who saw the person, and the rules discover nobody for
themselves. The advocate's duty to begin assistance starts once a person is in
the record, and the passerby's report can bring in someone no other record
reaches only once somebody writes it. Whether anyone sees the person, writes
the entry and then acts on the duties that follow is a question for the second
book.

The next chapter asks what else may enter this limited record about the person.

## Argument: Standing before identity

*The companion states these rules as [Article 1](https://dhilipsiva.dev/rights-nobody-has-to-earn/constitution/#article-1).*

I let standing follow from a birth or an encounter because a floor is tested by
the people least able to prove who they are: a newborn nobody registered, a
stateless adult, someone who arrives with nothing. A rule that waits for proof
of identity asks the person with the least to produce the one thing they are
least likely to hold, and every other promise waits behind it. International
law points the same way and stops at identity itself. Target 16.9 of the
Sustainable Development Goals pledges "legal identity for all, including birth
registration" by 2030, as a political commitment.[^sdg] The 1954 Convention
requires states parties to issue identity papers to stateless people in their
territory who lack a valid travel document,[^stateless-persons] and the 1961
Convention requires nationality for someone born in a state's territory who
would otherwise be stateless, though it permits conditions when nationality is
granted on application.[^statelessness-reduction] Each treats identity as
something to be provided to a person. I go one step further: nothing owed to
a person waits for those papers.

The strongest alternative is identity-first welfare: verify who a claimant is,
match the claim against a register, then pay. India wrote a version of it into
law: section 7 of the Aadhaar Act of 2016 lets a government require
authentication as a condition of a subsidy, with other means of identification
for anyone never assigned a number.[^aadhaar] Its case is serious. Money paid
to invented or duplicate claimants is money missing from genuine ones, and a
register is the direct way to find them. The evidence I cite measures its
costs, but read fairly it also helps the alternative. Muralidharan, Niehaus and
Sukhtankar's study of Jharkhand's biometric-authentication reforms attributes
much of the adverse effect it found to the protocols used for the
transition,[^lost-access] which leaves open that verification introduced with
better protocols would exclude far fewer people.

The two choices put their costs on different people. Identity-first welfare
puts them on genuine claimants who fail the match. In Jharkhand, 1.5 to 2
million legitimate beneficiaries lost access to benefits at some point during
the reforms,[^lost-access] and 88 per cent of the cancelled ration cards the
same authors sampled belonged to genuine households, a figure they call purely
descriptive.[^genuine-cards] My rule puts the cost on whoever funds provision,
and in a real shortage on other claimants: provision will sometimes reach a
duplicate or a false name, because the rules accept an encounter's name
without authenticating it. Duplicates can still be found afterwards, as
separate claims open to challenge. I would rather the error cost money than
cost someone a meal they were owed.

I would reconsider on either of two findings: that verification can run,
among many unregistered people, beside a fallback that keeps every legitimate
claimant served while identity is checked; or that standing by encounter lets
duplicate claims grow until genuine claimants go without essentials. Either
would reverse the comparison this choice rests on.
