# The Child With Nobody

The record supplied for this chapter says one thing about Nell, and only one
thing: that Nell was born. No entry names a parent, a family, a home, a
household, a supporter, a case, an order, or a teacher. Nothing in the record
says that anyone has done anything to Nell or for Nell. There is no note of
where Nell is, no note of who is there, no note of what Nell needs. There is
the birth, and there is silence.

This chapter asks what the rules make of that record. It is the shortest
question in the book and the one the rest of the book keeps returning to,
because a rule that protects a person about whom the record knows nothing
except that they exist is protecting them on the only ground it has, and a
rule that does not is waiting for an entry somebody may never write.

Two things need saying before the questions start. First, "nobody" here means
what it means everywhere in this book: an absence in the record. It is not a
claim about Nell's life, and it cannot be. The rules read entries, and where
there is no entry the rules read nothing. Second, every sentence below about
Nell is one of five kinds — an entry that is present, an entry that is absent,
a conclusion that derives, a conclusion that does not, or a rule stated. None of
them says what Nell feels, wants, or lacks. The record has no word for any of
that, and this chapter will not supply one.

Start with the birth. The rule that reads it is short: a recorded birth, for
anyone who is not a public body, makes a person. Nothing else is asked. The
rule does not read a parent, an age, a name, a registry, a document, or a
match against any list. So Nell is a person — not provisionally, not pending
anything, and not because somebody later confirmed it. The conclusion sits
directly on the entry, and the entry is the only one there is.

Ask the other questions the record could answer and it answers each of them
the same way. Does the record hold a family for Nell? No. A home? No. Is Nell
confined? No. Has Nell been released from anything? No. Has anyone's word about
Nell been voided, or anyone's mark on Nell been made clean? No. Does Nell hold
any office, or any recognition? No. Each of those is a plain absence, and each
would flip the instant a line were written. Hold onto that: a great deal of what
follows turns on which absences the rules read and which they do not.

## Everything owed

The floor is owed to every person. That sentence is not a summary of the rules;
it is their shape. Each debt the public duty carries — food, shelter, care,
learning, safety, expression, belief, company — is written as a rule whose
whole condition is that the person is a person. Nothing in any of those rules
reads an age. Nothing reads a parent. Nothing reads a household, a registry, a
document, or any entry beyond the one that made Nell a person in the first
place.

So Nell is owed everything. Not a child's share, not a provisional share until
somebody claims Nell, not a share conditioned on a relative turning up. The
whole floor, in full, on the strength of a birth.

The chapter's pins enumerate this rather than sampling it, and the reason is
worth stating. A universal that is checked on one item is a universal that has
been checked on one item. The place a universal fails is the item nobody
thought to ask about, so every debt is asked about by name, and every
entitlement beside it. There is no item on the floor for which the record
answers differently about Nell than it would about anyone else.

Notice what has not been done to reach this. No one classified Nell. No one
assessed Nell's needs, weighed Nell's prospects, or entered a status that
marked Nell out for protection. A design that first had to decide who is
vulnerable would have built, in the same stroke, the instrument for deciding
who is not; this design reads personhood and stops. The word *vulnerable* is
not merely absent from Nell's record. It is not a word the record can hold at
all, and the pins show it being refused before it can load. Whatever protects
Nell is not a finding about Nell.

## No one is presumed to provide

A society can owe a child everything and still leave the child with nothing, if
the rules quietly assume that somebody else is providing it. The commonest way
to assume that is to presume a family. This design refuses the presumption in
writing, and it refuses it for a person exactly like Nell.

The barriers the life-course rules raise are barriers on public power and on
anyone who would act in its name, and almost all of them have the same
condition: that the person is a person. Personhood alone puts every one of
them in place. No family, household, association, or charity is presumed to
supply the floor. Public care continuity is immediate and cannot be delegated
away. Kinship creates no personal-care debt that someone else's absence could
leave unpaid. A child holds rights independently, is heard without any minimum
speaking age, and is given information and help in a form the child can use.
Separation from whatever care exists is a last resort, and support comes before
any removal. A missing relationship — no parent on the record, no household on
the record — creates no independence finding and no ground for confinement. The
household is not prescribed, no status raises a presumption of harm, and a care
status never stands in for care actually delivered. Where the constitution's
knowledge stops and the operating world begins is itself one of the barriers,
so that nobody can read a rule here as proof that something happened out there.

Every one of those holds for Nell, and the pins say so by name. They hold not
because Nell was recognised as a child in need but because Nell is a person, and
that is the whole of what they read.

A few barriers in the same family do read something beside personhood: a
recorded family, a recorded home, a marriage or a sibling. They exist to forbid
substituting that status for a finding. Where the same barrier is also written
over personhood alone elsewhere in the design, it holds for Nell through that
route; where it reads a marriage or a sibling and nothing else, there is no
entry for it to be about, and it does not. That is not a gap in Nell's
protection. A rule that forbids reading a status that is not there has nothing
to forbid.

There is one more thing the barriers do not do, and it matters as much as what
they do. None of them is read by any other rule. Nothing takes a barrier as a
premise and concludes that a placement happened, that care arrived, or that a
child was heard. They are limits, and a limit is honoured by nothing following
from its breach — which is the honest shape, and the shape the next section
has to reckon with.

## The duty that needs no family in the record

Underneath the barriers there is an ordinary case, and Nell is the reason to
look at it. The life-course rules carry a duty of immediate care continuity
that is public, cannot be delegated, and needs nobody to hold any role. The
record for that duty completes with no family in it at all — no parent, no
caregiver, no household, no supporter — and it confers no role over the person
on whoever carries it. That is the design's answer to the child with nobody,
stated as a rule rather than a hope: the continuity is not conditioned on a
relationship, on a supporter, or on an entry somebody has to make first.

The pins for that case live with the family's own tests, and the header of this
chapter's pin file names them. They establish that the duty derives, that it
derives without any role record, and that the actor who carries it acquires no
parentage and no standing of their own from carrying it.

They also establish, in the same breath, everything the duty is not. The family
ships dormant: in the record supplied with this book, no continuity record is
written for anyone, so no continuity duty derives for anyone, and none derives
for Nell. And even where the record is written, a duty that derives is a duty
that derives. It does not prove that anyone arrived, that care continued, that
the child was reached, or that the actor named in the record exists. The
design can say, with complete precision, what is owed to a child with nobody
and who is bound to provide it. It cannot say, from that alone, that anybody
did.

## What does not follow

Now the half this book will not soften, and it is sharpest here.

Ask whether Nell eats in the record supplied for this chapter. Not whether Nell
is owed food — that was settled above — but whether the record concludes that
food reached Nell. It does not. Shelter? No. Care? No. Safety, company,
learning? No, no, and no. Each of those is a floor actuality, and a floor
actuality derives in exactly two ways: through confinement, which is the one
route the shipped record actually exercises, or through a record that
something reached this particular person, attested independently by a witness
authorised for that person who was not the one who gave it. Nell is not
confined. Nell has no receipt. Nell has no witness. So nothing derives.

The mirror is worth holding up. The pinned portfolio carries a person, never
convicted of anything, for whom every recipient-side actuality derives: a
receipt for each item, an authorised inspector who is not the provider, the
same kind of delivery attested on both sides. Take away the receipts and the
witness and that person's record looks like Nell's. The routes are not blind
and they are not dead. They are waiting for entries that, in Nell's record,
nobody has written.

Learning is the same. The older route derives it when teaching was heard; Nell
has no teacher on the record and nothing heard. Belief and expression
deliberately have no delivery route at all — belief because certifying it would
build the instrument its protection exists to refuse, and expression because a
recorded voice derives only for someone public power physically holds. Nell is
held by nobody.

Say precisely what this is and is not. It is not a finding that Nell is hungry,
unsheltered, uncared for, unsafe, or alone. The record has no words for any of
those, and would refuse them if offered. It is a statement about what the
supplied record can establish, and the statement is complete: the record does
not say that Nell ate. It does not say that Nell did not eat. It says nothing,
and the rules derive nothing from nothing.

That is the whole shape of the floor, seen from the one place it is easiest to
see. Owed is not the same as delivered. The design can hold the entitlement
unconditional and the barriers absolute and still be unable to say, about the
person it protects most completely on paper, that a single thing arrived.
Every later chapter that shows a route working should be read against this
one, because this is the record every route has to be able to reach.

## The child the record never entered

Nell has one entry. Ori has none.

Ask about Ori and the record answers no to everything. Ori is not a person. Ori
is owed nothing. Ori is entitled to nothing. No barrier holds for Ori, no duty
runs to Ori, and no liberty is Ori's. Every one of those answers is a real
answer and not an empty one: each would flip the moment a birth or an
encounter were written. Until that line is written, the design does not know
Ori exists.

This is the boundary of what a design like this can do, and it is stated
rather than hidden. The rules read entries. A child who has never been entered
is not a child the rules failed; the rules never saw the child. The entry
routes are as wide as the design could make them — a birth, a first contact,
presence within reach, effective control, any of them enough, none of them
needing a registry match — but every route is a route in, and nothing sits
outside the record noticing who has not come in. The design's own alarms
confirm it: the marker that watches company watches the confined, and the
marker somebody might one day build to watch for undelivered food would fire
for Nell and for everyone else alive in the record, for the same reason, and
never for Ori.

There is a second boundary, one rule wide. The chapter's counterfactual deletes
the birth rule and runs Nell's record again. Nell is still born. Nell is no
longer a person. And with personhood gone, everything this chapter derived goes
with it in the same instant: nothing owed, nothing entitled, no barrier in
place, no liberty, and no alarm. The record does not object. Nothing fails,
nothing warns. A design could run in that state indefinitely without any part
of it noticing that its newborns had ceased to count, because every duty and
every barrier reads personhood and personhood would have nothing to derive
from. That is what "everything rests on one line" means when it is measured:
the line is short, it is load-bearing, and its removal is silent.

## What this chapter does not establish

It establishes no birth. The record was given a birth entry; nothing observed
one, and nothing authenticates the entry or the person who wrote it. It
establishes no registry, and it needs none — that is the point of the entry
routes — but it also proves nothing about how a birth comes to be recorded in
any operating world.

It does not establish that anyone was authorised to witness anything for Nell,
that a receipt exists, or that any of the floor arrived. It does not establish
that a care continuity record was written, that the duty it would create was
carried, or that a child was reached. Nothing here proves an offer, an
accessible route, an adequate one, or an operation of any kind. Those belong to
the second book.

And it does not establish the argument for why this case matters — why a rule
that protects Nell protects everyone. That argument is not a rule and no machine
checked it. It is the author's, and it is made in the opening note, where
argument is allowed to stand as argument. This chapter has only shown what the
rules do with one entry and what they do with none: everything, and nothing,
and the line that decides which.
