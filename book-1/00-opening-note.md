# A Note Before the Design

*A constitution designed from the person with nothing, argued in plain
language, with every rule published so you can test it.*

In March 2017 the state of Jharkhand, in India, ordered that ration cards not
linked to Aadhaar, the national biometric identity number, be cancelled, and
cards were cancelled in large numbers, eligible families' among
them.[^jharkhand] Santoshi Kumari's family was one of them: its card was
cancelled and its rations were refused. On 28 September 2017 Santoshi, who was
eleven, died in Simdega district. Her family said she died asking for rice; the
district administration said she died of malaria.[^santoshi] That dispute is
unresolved, and this book leaves it so. The order of events is not in dispute:
a record failed to match, an eligible family's entitlement stopped, and the
rations stopped with it.

This book asks what a society must provide for a person who can offer it
nothing in return, and how that person can hold it to the promise. I propose a
constitution in which being a person gives standing in law and a claim to
essentials. Employment, wealth, citizenship, family and good behaviour are not
entrance requirements. Food, shelter, care, learning, bodily safety, material
security, expression, belief and company form a **floor**: entitlements that
neither private dependence nor public punishment may withdraw, owed by named
public bodies. Above it, people choose lives the constitution does not
prescribe. The rest of the design follows from protecting that floor and the
people who hold it: government divided by place and function, the vote resting
on residence, records bound to their purpose, emergencies that suspend no
right, and a small core no majority may amend away. Each choice has costs, and
each chapter closes by arguing for its rule against the strongest alternative.

The first case is a child with nobody: a test record holding a birth entry and
nothing else. No parent, household or other provider is recorded, no supporter
has spoken for the child, and no public act has brought the child into an
official case. The test asks whether protection waits for any of those things,
and who must begin help when the person cannot ask. The rules answer that
standing follows from a birth, a first contact, presence, effective control or
a report that nobody is acting for someone, without documents or a family, and
that a named office owes initiation without a request. My argument is that this
is a good place to begin designing for everyone: where a right follows from
personhood alone, the entries other people possess cannot become conditions
for it. That wider claim is an argument, not a theorem about every harm.

The prisoner supplies the paired test. Public power has acted on this person.
Does the same floor survive confinement? Can the person contest the restriction
and leave when authority ends? The child tests what begins without a request;
the prisoner tests what remains under power. Neither is a model of ordinary
life, and *An Ordinary Week* shows how lightly the design touches a person the
record has no reason to decide anything about.

Three distinctions hold on every page, with one limit beside them, and the
chapters leave them unstated.

- **Record and world.** The rules read records. An absent entry is silence
  rather than a finding, and a false entry yields a false conclusion as
  reliably as a true one yields a true one. The rules check that evidence is
  present and properly made, and leave its honesty to people who can
  investigate.
- **Entitlement and delivery.** An entitlement names a debt: who owes it and
  what evidence would show that it arrived. Whether it arrived is a separate
  fact.
- **Conclusion and event.** A conclusion that a review is owed or that
  authority has ended is a statement of law. The review, the release and the
  meal are events someone must make happen, and a duty stays owed until it is.
- **Operation belongs to the second book.** Whether offices are staffed,
  services funded and duties carried out is the subject of *What It Would
  Take*; this volume specifies the destination.

The cases attribute no fear, comfort or hope to their named people, and any
feelings you imagine belong to your reading rather than to their evidence.

The book claims two things: that its rules have the consequences it describes
for the records it gives them, which executable tests check, and that its
choices can be defended against their strongest alternatives, which it argues.
It does not claim that the society operates or is affordable, that consistency
makes the rules just, or that any test proves them right. Part V draws the
arguments together, names what the book contributes, chief among them keeping
entitlement, duty, evidence and delivery apart, and ranks where the design
could fail. The ideas it builds on are credited where they are used, from Henry
Shue's case for subsistence as a basic right to Amartya Sen's account of hunger
as a failure of entitlement.

I speak directly in this note, in the documented case that opens each Part, in
the labelled argument that closes each chapter, in Part V and in the optional
method; every other section states the consequences of the formal design.

This book was written with AI assistance throughout. The design's rulings are
mine. An AI assistant drafted the prose of the chapters, their arguments,
Part V, the method, this note and the English rendering of the epigraph, and I
approved it, much of it under a standing approval of recommended revisions
rather than passage by passage. The same sessions wrote the formal rules that
express the rulings, their tests, the generators and the reader's companion,
and located and checked the sources. The rules run on Nibli, an engine I also
maintain, so the constitution, its tests and the engine share a maintainer, and
their agreement is a check within one project rather than an independent
confirmation. That work is checked mechanically and partially. The
consequences each chapter states are paired with executable tests, which run in
full whenever the rules change; core cases have been replayed in a second
engine through a translation made here; rules have been deliberately broken to
see whether a test notices; quoted rules must match the source; figures in the
arguments must match a registry entry recording what was read; and the prose is
measured for the vocabulary it may use. None of that shows the arguments are
good, and no independent expert has reviewed the whole design.

**Begin with [Chapter 1: The Child With Nobody](01-the-child-with-nobody.md).**
No glossary or technical preparation is needed. Read the cases in order through
Part V. If you want the reasons before the institutional detail, the
[map of choices](reference.md#the-choices-and-their-arguments) at the back
leads to them, beside the glossary and index. Continue to the method only if
you want to see how the rules are run and tested. The current text and a
companion that runs selected cases in your browser are at
[dhilipsiva.dev/rights-nobody-has-to-earn](https://dhilipsiva.dev/rights-nobody-has-to-earn/);
its answers concern the records given to it, not what any society delivered.
