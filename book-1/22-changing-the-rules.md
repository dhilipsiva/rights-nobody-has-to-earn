# Changing the Rules

The record holds a proposal that the Assembly placed on its docket and the
electorate approved, and the proposal declares that it changes the article
protecting the floor. The rules conclude no change in the law from those
entries: the entries record a decision about a proposal, and they hold no
replacement text that could be reviewed, published or put in force.

Changing a constitution requires agreement on both the decision and the text
being changed. Approval of a proposal extends only to its own exact replacement,
and a technically compatible replacement still needs that approval. The design
therefore distinguishes political authority, certification of an exact
candidate, publication and the version in force.

The political route requires a current record for the exact proposal: a
full-membership Assembly result, a national referendum result, any required
Regions Council and affected-region consent, separate completeness assurance,
constitutional review, challenge, correction and a current certificate.
The thresholds are two-thirds of the full Assembly membership and more
affirmative than negative valid referendum votes, with no turnout quorum; a tie
fails. Additional regional approvals apply where the federal settlement is
directly affected.

Those are legal requirements over recorded results. Counting the electorate,
authenticating each submission and finding any voter left out belong to the
offices whose certificates the rules read. The resulting authority is bounded
to the recorded proposal and current source; identifying, publishing and
installing a replacement are separate stages.

## Certification, publication and effect

The exact-change route connects the political result to the existing text, the
proposed replacement and the transition between them. Independent
compatibility review must concern the same candidate, and its review of effects
must match that replacement, including any vocabulary change. Consent and
compatibility are separate premises, and each is required.

| Stage | What the record shows | What still needs separate evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Certified candidate | The exact proposed replacement meets the authority and review conditions. | Authorised publication. |
| Published candidate | Publication is bound to that certified candidate. | Selection as the version in force. |
| Version in force | The chosen transition matches the current base and exact replacement. | Truth of the recorded evidence and actual institutional operation. |

A candidate needs consent that matches it exactly. A different candidate
inserted into an upstream record matches nothing downstream and makes that
record ambiguous. When two selections compete, both are marked as a conflict
and neither candidate comes into force. A transition binds the base it was made
for, so replayed against a later base it certifies nothing. Returning to
earlier wording needs fresh authority and a new transition while preserving the
intervening record.

Anyone may request independent review without the operator's permission or a
valid candidate certificate. The request binds the office authorised to hear
such disputes, whenever someone other than that office makes it. A current reviewed source defect withholds certification,
publication and the version in force, even from a text already in force, and
obliges the operator to preserve the amendment history and correct the
affected use; every person's floor remains owed throughout. Only a positively
certified failure to respond moves the review duty to an independent alternate,
and silence counts as neither consent nor a finding against the requester.
Correcting a source leaves each individual judgment and remedy to its own
process.

Whether publication occurred and an institution adopted the replacement belongs
to the second book. The
[method](method.md#comparing-and-selecting-an-amendment) distinguishes the
formal evidence requirements from the separate comparison of exact text and
version changes.

## The protected core

The protected core is what every amendment must preserve, and the rules name
its provisions in a closed list: universal standing, the floor, equal
protection, due process, core liberties, the constraints protecting the
commons, the categorical refusals on the use of force, the bans on sending
anyone into persecution or torture and on collective expulsion, prompt
independent review of detention, a remedy that works, and the capacity of the
Assembly and the Constitutional Court to sit. Three protections for animals
complete it: a credibly sentient animal's direct protected-subject status, the
ban on severe avoidable suffering, and the ban on killing solely for one of the
dispensable purposes the rules list. The food rule and the detailed standards
are constitutional law that an amendment may change inside those three, and a
food purpose authorises no avoidable suffering.

The economic constitution entrenches no single ownership form, provider mix,
market share, profit, bailout, immunity or monopoly. Democratic law may change
economic instruments above the floor, and every such change must preserve the
core.

Exact-change certification requires positive review of the whole core, and a
separate positive review of each of the three animal protections. A finding that
a candidate removes an animal's direct status or weakens either ban must be
independently reviewed and must compare the actual base, candidate and effects,
tied to the proposal and transition; a text label or an unreviewed allegation is
no such finding. The finding holds against any majority, emergency, profit,
custom, claimed necessity or generic statement of compatibility. It blocks
certification, authorised publication and selection as the version in force, and
it obliges the responding office to refuse the candidate and secure an
independent court challenge.

A reviewer's reading of the core refuses a candidate when it gives its grounds:
which listed provision is at stake, the change in the candidate that reaches
it, and the reasons. A contrary reading without those grounds withholds
certification exactly as a missing positive review would, and the record stays
open to a decision. Anyone may ask the independent final compatibility review
for that decision, and the request obliges it to decide. The final reviewer
stands apart from the record's attesters, its operator, the political bodies
and whoever administered the result. Its finding that the candidate is
compatible stands in for the reviewer's positive reading and answers a reasoned
refusal, while every other field of the record still needs both attesters. An
independently found animal-core breach still blocks certification whatever the
final review finds. A reading of the core, however wide, creates no right; it
decides only whether this candidate proceeds.

Refusal reaches this candidate alone. Discussion of it, challenge, everyone's
existing human rights, animals' care and the lawful history of amendment all
continue, and the breach finding makes their preservation a duty. The refusal
is a conclusion over the recorded evidence of the candidate's effects, so the
rules can miss a change concealed from that evidence.

## What a proposal entry establishes

A name on a docket records a proposal. A declared target records what it says
it changes. An approval entry records an outcome. None supplies the exact
candidate, current political certificate, independent effect review, publication
or selection as the version in force required above.

Whatever target an approved proposal declares, a protected article, an ordinary
one or none at all, its entries confer no status as law. The register of
protected names lists the floor article, prisoner personhood and the register
itself, and it reads neither a candidate's text nor its effects. A copy of the
source edited outside this route has the consequences of its edited text and
none of the route's authority: a replacement comes into force only after its
actual text and vocabulary have been reviewed.

## People and proposal names

A proposal decision concerns the proposal and its candidate text. A personal
finding needs its own legal grounds and process. Suppose a person's name is
placed on the docket as a proposal, with a protected target declared and an
approval recorded for it, and suppose the same person has a recorded
contribution. Those entries produce no credibility finding against the person,
and the recorded contribution identifies the act and confers no general
recognition status. A proposal that declares the protected floor as its target
likewise receives no personal credibility finding.

Authority to amend comes from the exact-change route alone, through
certification, publication and selection as the version in force, and a bare
proposal entry completes none of its stages.

## What entrenchment requires

An unamendable core limits even a large majority and binds later participants
who did not choose it. They bear its cost: a majority that wants to change what
the core protects, however large and however lasting, has no lawful route to
that change. Everything outside the core, from economic instruments to the food
rule and the institutions themselves, stays open to the same demanding route,
whose cost falls on reformers whose proposal has majority support but falls
short of two-thirds of the full Assembly.

The route holds only where each connection holds: the political result includes
the entitled electorate, approval concerns the candidate actually reviewed,
review protects the core, and the resulting text is the version in force. A
register of target names answers none of those questions.

The integrity of the record those rules are written in also remains necessary.
Anyone with access to the source file can change it outside the authorised
process. The rules deny such an edit authority but leave it physically
possible, and who made it and what it changes lie outside what they read.

The next chapters turn to what the design may do to a person, beginning with
protection for someone who exposes authority.

## Argument: A fixed core behind an open route

*The companion states these rules as Articles [23](https://dhilipsiva.dev/rights-nobody-has-to-earn/constitution/#article-23) and [30](https://dhilipsiva.dev/rights-nobody-has-to-earn/constitution/#article-30).*

I put a small core beyond amendment because a majority able to withdraw
standing, the floor, due process or a working remedy from its opponents could
change who takes part in the next decision, and animals have no ballot with
which to resist. Everything else stays amendable, because institutions are
means and later generations must be able to correct them. The route asks for
two-thirds of the full Assembly, so an ordinary governing majority cannot change
the rules alone, and a national referendum, so representatives cannot change
them without the voters. It has no turnout quorum, because a quorum lets
opponents defeat a proposal by staying home. I review the exact text for its
effects because approval attaches to words, while the core protects what the
words do.

Constitutions have drawn this line before, in their text and by judicial
implication, as Yaniv Roznai's comparative study of amendment limits sets
out.[^roznai] Germany's Basic Law bars any amendment affecting the division into
Länder, their participation in principle in legislation, or the principles of
its Articles 1 and 20, among them human dignity, basic rights binding the state,
and a democratic and social federal state under the rule of law.[^art79] In
Kesavananda Bharati India's Supreme Court held, by a narrow majority, that
Parliament's power to amend does not include power to alter the Constitution's
basic structure.[^kesavananda] In 2010 Colombia's Constitutional Court struck
down a law calling a referendum on a second presidential re-election, for
procedural defects and because the change would replace structural axes of the
Constitution, among them alternation in office and the separation of
powers.[^c141] David Landau reports that the decision was complied with and
"probably did prevent a significant erosion of democracy".[^landau]

The strongest alternative is a fully revisable constitution. Jeremy Waldron
argues that where democratic and judicial institutions work and people
committed to rights disagree about them, representatives should settle the
disagreement, and judges should not strike down their laws.[^waldron] Its best
evidence comes from Landau himself, who finds the doctrine of unconstitutional
amendments often hard to deploy against would-be autocrats, or easy for them to
avoid.

My choice costs a later majority, however large and lasting, that wants to
change what the core protects. It gives reviewers power over whether a reform
crosses the line, which is why a reading refuses only when it names the
provision, the change and its reasons, and an independent final review can
answer it. Reformers with majority support can be blocked by more than a third
of the Assembly. Elkins, Ginsburg and Melton find constitutions imperilled by
crises, with design able to lower their risk of death;[^elkins] a core too
rigid for its society may be replaced rather than amended.

I would reconsider if readings of the core repeatedly blocked reforms that kept
its protections whole, if the route repeatedly stalled rights-preserving
reforms with majority support, or if constitutions without a fixed core
proved as reliable at keeping the losers of a political contest able to live,
dissent and seek correction.
